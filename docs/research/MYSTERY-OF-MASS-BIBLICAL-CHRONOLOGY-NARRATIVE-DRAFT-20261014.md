# Mystery of the Mass — Biblical chronology: source-led first narrative draft

**Editorial review manuscript · 14 October 2026. Not approved, translated or published.**

This is **not** a new Bible summary and is **not** a liturgical rewrite. The 24 research scenes are the first connected prose synthesis drawn from the existing original-text research, especially St Alphonsus's source-located argument, Gihr, Guéranger, Le Brun and the historical meditative treatments, with the biblical text primary and the 1962 Mass carefully distinguished.

Each scene may later be collapsed or expanded. The research classification of a connection is retained: direct text or named figure, traditional typology, a doctrinal synthesis, or a specific author's meditation. Reference paragraphs are research prose, **not quotations** from authors.

The companion [liturgical Study evidence reader](MYSTERY-OF-MASS-LITURGICAL-CHRONOLOGY-STUDY-READING-20261014.md) is a different journey; it follows the Mass and incorporates the previously developed `Study of the Mass` explanations. The [machine-readable draft](../../data/learn/research/mystery-mass-source-led-narrative-drafts-20261014.v1.json) retains all evidence links and French manuscript selections.


## 01. Creation and worship

The problem is not why man invented sacrifice, but why the creature can owe anything to the Creator. St Alphonsus distinguishes the ends traditionally attributed to sacrifice—adoration, thanksgiving, expiation and petition—and the acts by which an offering is made. Creation makes the first two intelligible; the Fall will make the third urgent. The Eucharist is not simply the most impressive instance of an older ritual category. It is Christ's own offering, which gives the Church's thanksgiving and petition their efficacy.

**The movement to the next scene:** At Creation, worship is an obligation and a privilege; after sin, it will also become a plea for reconciliation.

**Scripture:** Genesis 1–2. The app's shared Bible reader must supply the longer passage, chapter context and controlled edition.

## 02. Fall and promised redemption

The Fall introduces a contradiction into human worship: the creature still owes God honour, yet can no longer approach Him as though nothing had happened. Dujardin reads the opening movement of Mass against Adam's loss of innocence. His interpretation is a spiritual lens, not the original meaning of the Roman opening prayers. The actual Confiteor and Kyrie speak of guilt and mercy, while Genesis establishes why those themes cannot be detached from the history of redemption.

**The movement to the next scene:** The first sacrifices will expose the question the Fall leaves unresolved: what makes an offering acceptable to God?

**Scripture:** Genesis 3 · Luke 18:13. The app's shared Bible reader must supply the longer passage, chapter context and controlled edition.

## 03. Abel: sacrifice, righteousness and innocent blood

Abel's offering receives divine favour; the text does not explain its acceptance through a technical ritual formula. Hebrews interprets his offering in terms of faith. Gihr, reading Abel through the Roman Canon, develops a typology of the righteous offerer and the innocent blood that cries out. We must not adopt his additional conjecture that fire descended from heaven: Genesis does not say so. When Supra quae names Abel after the Consecration, it asks us to consider divine acceptance, not equality between an ancient gift and Christ Himself.

**The movement to the next scene:** The problem of accepted sacrifice persists, but the next figure introduces a stranger element: priesthood without the genealogy of Israel's later priests.

## 04. Melchisedech: bread, wine and priesthood

In Genesis, Melchisedech is both king and priest; he brings forth bread and wine and blesses Abraham. The Epistle to the Hebrews returns to the brief episode to explain a priesthood fulfilled in Christ. Gihr interprets the bread and wine sacrificially, following a long Christian tradition, but Genesis 14 does not itself narrate their consecration or explicitly state that they were immolated. This distinction matters because the Mass invokes Melchisedech as a figure of the Eucharistic offering without making his rite identical with the Roman rite.

**The movement to the next scene:** The mysterious priest meets Abraham before the more painful trial in which Abraham must relinquish the son of promise.

## 05. Abraham and Isaac: son of the promise

The command to offer Isaac challenges the promise made to Abraham: the son through whom the covenant is to continue appears to be demanded back. Abraham is stopped, and Isaac is not killed. Gihr and St Alphonsus read the obedience of Abraham and the beloved son carrying wood as prefiguring Christ, but this becomes intelligible only because the outcomes differ: Isaac is spared while Christ freely undergoes death. Supra quae later names Abraham's sacrifice among the accepted offerings, while the Eucharist presents the one infinitely greater sacrifice.

**The movement to the next scene:** What Abraham undergoes privately, Israel will next encounter nationally: deliverance bound to a lamb and its blood.

## 06. Passover: blood of deliverance

In Exodus 12 the blood of a lamb identifies the households spared in the judgment upon Egypt. The meal is bound to a commanded remembrance and to the departure from bondage. St Alphonsus expressly uses the Paschal lamb among the Old Law's anticipations of Christ's sacrifice. The Christian claim is not that the Roman Agnus Dei simply quotes Exodus, but that Christ is the Paschal Lamb whose death accomplishes a deeper deliverance; the Gospel and St Paul provide the bridge.

**The movement to the next scene:** The people saved through the sea must still be sustained on their journey; redemption calls for nourishment, not deliverance alone.

## 07. Manna: food in the wilderness

The manna is a gift for a pilgrim people, neither earned nor supplied by their own resources. Yet those who ate it still died. In John 6, Christ sets the true Bread from heaven in deliberate contrast with the manna: His gift is not merely provisional bodily sustenance. The Eucharistic application must be presented through Christ's discourse and Catholic sacramental teaching rather than pretending that Exodus already describes the consecrated Host. This stage prepares Communion, without collapsing the distinction between Israel's history and the sacrament.

**The movement to the next scene:** Food for the journey is followed by covenant: a relationship formally sealed with blood.

**Scripture:** Exodus 16 · John 6:31–58. The app's shared Bible reader must supply the longer passage, chapter context and controlled edition.

## 08. Sinai: covenant sealed in blood

At Sinai, the people assent to the words of the covenant and Moses applies blood in its solemn confirmation. Jeremiah promises a new covenant, not simply a renewal of compliance with the former one. At the Last Supper Christ speaks of His Blood in connection with the new covenant. St Alphonsus reads this in light of the Eucharistic institution and its sacrificial fulfilment. The chalice formula of the 1962 Roman Missal has its own received wording; it is not a quotation copied without change from one biblical account.

**The movement to the next scene:** The covenant also requires an ordered life of worship: altar, sanctuary, priest and appointed sacrifice.

## 09. Sanctuary and ministerial priesthood

The tabernacle and the later Temple teach that access to God's presence is never treated as a casual activity. Priestly mediation and sacrifice are part of Israel's worship, but the Letter to the Hebrews explains both their dignity and their insufficiency compared with Christ's priesthood. St Alphonsus uses Levitical figures, while later commentators sometimes connect the priest's extended hands at Hanc igitur with the imposition of hands upon a victim. This is symbolic interpretation, not a demonstrable explanation of the gesture's Roman origin. The live scapegoat of Leviticus 16 is sent away rather than slain.

**The movement to the next scene:** The prophet who sees God's throne hears a hymn that the Roman Mass will actually take into its own words.

## 10. Isaiah's heavenly worship

Isaiah's vision begins not with a description of a temple object but with the holiness of the Lord before whom even the seraphim veil themselves. The Sanctus draws upon this proclamation, joined in Christian worship with the acclamation addressed to Christ on His entry into Jerusalem. The two sources should remain visible. The liturgical text speaks with Scripture's language but is not a continuous quotation from a single episode. The difference between vision and liturgical adaptation is itself a fruitful matter for contemplation.

**The movement to the next scene:** The same prophet also announces a suffering servant; heavenly holiness will be approached through the sufferings of the innocent.

## 11. Suffering Servant: the innocent victim

Isaiah's suffering servant bears the iniquities of many without simply being one more animal substitute. St Alphonsus considers such prophecies within the older law's sacrificial figures and turns to Hebrews to explain their fulfilment. The decisive claim of Christian sacrificial theology is that Christ's obedience and self-offering exceed every previous victim. The narrative must let the biblical passage speak in its own context before explaining the Church's interpretation of it as a prophecy of the Passion and its relation to Mass.

## 12. Prophecy of the pure offering

Malachi's announcement of a pure offering among the nations belongs first to the prophet's rebuke of corrupt worship. Christian tradition reads its universality and purity in relation to the Eucharistic Sacrifice. Le Brun's preliminary treatise specifically moves from the sacrifices of the old dispensation to Christ, their fulfilment, and then to the Mass. This is a theological reading within tradition, not the claim that the prophet described the Roman Missal's future gestures or textual formulas.

**The movement to the next scene:** The promise now ceases to be merely a figure or oracle: the divine Son assumes the nature in which He can offer Himself.

## 13. Annunciation and Incarnation

At the Annunciation, the eternal Son becomes man without ceasing to be God. St Alphonsus treats the Incarnation as the preparation of the victim who will offer Himself freely: a human body is taken for the accomplishment of the Father's will, as Hebrews interprets the mystery. This does not mean the historical sacrifice of Calvary is already finished at Nazareth. The Mass's Credo and the Last Gospel confess the Incarnation in different ritual positions, but the salvation-history journey encounters it at the historical beginning of Christ's earthly life.

**The movement to the next scene:** The birth of the Word in the flesh is greeted with praise that will become the opening words of a much longer liturgical hymn.

## 14. Nativity and angelic Gloria

The Gloria begins with the proclamation heard by the shepherds at Bethlehem. St Alphonsus notes the scriptural opening, but the hymn that follows is the Church's developed act of praise and petition, not an unaltered transcript of the angels' song. The Nativity therefore contributes actual language to the Mass while the later liturgical text expands its scope to adoration of the Trinity and supplication to the Lamb. This is a stronger connection than treating the priest's posture as an imitation of a shepherd.

**The movement to the next scene:** The child announced as Saviour will be identified publicly as the Lamb whose sacrifice removes sin.

## 15. John the Baptist calls Christ the Lamb

Their location before Communion belongs to the liturgical arrangement; their theological force comes from the identity of the sacrificed and living Christ.

## 17. Entry into Jerusalem and Benedictus

The crowd's cry when Christ enters Jerusalem includes 'Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord.' Within the Sanctus the Church places that acclamation after Isaiah's heavenly hymn. The juxtaposition matters: worshippers bless the coming Messiah while joining the worship of heaven. But the Mass does not present Palm Sunday as though it occurred after Isaiah's vision in one biblical narrative. The two biblical moments are joined liturgically; in this journey, they remain chronologically distinct.

**The movement to the next scene:** The Lord's arrival leads to the institution of the Eucharist in anticipation of His Passion.

## 18. The Last Supper and Eucharistic institution

The evangelists and St Paul witness Christ giving bread and wine as His Body and Blood and commanding the memorial. St Alphonsus treats the Last Supper in explicit relation to sacrifice and the Church's Eucharistic participation; he also distinguishes his own conclusion from an anonymous French writer's account of sacrificial consummation. A serious treatment must preserve this disagreement rather than smoothing it into a single inherited explanation. The sacramental institution anticipates and is inseparable from Calvary, without multiplying historical sacrifices.

**The movement to the next scene:** The freedom of Christ's offering becomes manifest when He accepts the Father's will in Gethsemane.

## 19. Gethsemane and voluntary oblation

Gethsemane shows that Christ's sacrifice is voluntary obedience, not merely suffering inflicted by others. Hebrews interprets the saving efficacy of His offering through the obedience of His will. St Alphonsus traces the sacrificial purpose of the Incarnation toward this consent and its fulfilment; the Passion-meditation methods of later writers associate particular Mass gestures with this scene. Such associations may aid prayer, but they must remain optional, explicitly attributed, and separate from the historical cause of the gestures.

**The movement to the next scene:** The obedience accepted in the garden is consummated on Calvary, where all the earlier sacrificial figures are judged by their fulfilment.

## 20. Calvary: the unique and perfect sacrifice

At Calvary, the true Priest is also the Victim. St Alphonsus sets out the actions traditionally analysed in sacrifice and insists on Christ's once-for-all self-offering, then explains the Eucharist as sacramental participation in that same offering. He argues specifically against locating the fulfilment of every sacrificial act solely in Christ's glorification; the Eucharistic Communion matters to his account. Le Brun likewise makes Christ's unique sacrifice the point toward which prior sacrifices converge. In Mass the one offering is present sacramentally, not repeated as another execution.

**The movement to the next scene:** The Resurrection does not cancel the Cross; it reveals that the offering of the crucified Lord is victorious and enduring.

## 21. Resurrection and Emmaus

The risen Christ opens the Scriptures to the disciples on the road to Emmaus. Their retrospective understanding parallels the reader's task in the biblical journey: to discern the fulfilment of the earlier figures without rewriting them as if their authors had already seen every later rite. The Canon explicitly remembers the Resurrection in Unde et memores. The connection between Emmaus and Eucharistic Communion is a traditional interpretive reading, but the breaking of bread in Luke 24 cannot by itself be treated as a documentary account of the Roman Mass.

## 23. Pentecost and the Church's Eucharistic life

Pentecost forms a worshipping, teaching Church. The primitive community is described as persevering in the apostles' teaching, fellowship, breaking of bread and prayers. In the Roman Canon, the petitions for the living and the remembrance of saints make sense within this ecclesial unity. Dujardin meditates on Pentecost near the end of Mass, but that spiritual association does not establish an apostolic origin for the priest's final blessing. The Church's Eucharistic life is historical; the private meditation is interpretive.
