## The earliest directly inspected text: De sacramentis IV–V

**Direct Latin:** [De sacramentis, attributed to Ambrose, PL 16](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/De_sacramentis_(Ambrosius)), Book IV chs. 5–6 §§21–27 and Book V chs. 1–4.  
**Contemporary-of-the-early-rite source-critical interpretation:** [Dictionnaire de théologie catholique, *Messe VIII*, section VII, 1929, pp. 37–38](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_de_th%C3%A9ologie_catholique/MESSE_VIII._La_messe_dans_la_liturgie_7._Le_De_sacramentis_du_Pseudo_Ambroise).

The author of *De sacramentis* reports an early Eucharistic consecratory prayer, the commemoration of Passion, Resurrection and Ascension, and an appeal to accept gifts at the heavenly altar through angels, recalling Abel, Abraham and Melchizedek (IV.6.27). This is a major **early textual witness** to biblical material in later Roman liturgical prayer. It is **not a copy of the 1962 Roman Canon**.

In the transmitted *De sacramentis* wording, the heavenly-altar and angelic petition and the named patriarchal offerings form one prayer in a **different order** from the later *Supra quae* followed by *Supplices te rogamus*. The text says *per manus angelorum tuorum* (plural) rather than the received Roman *per manus sancti Angeli tui* (singular). The historical dictionary makes this textual difference explicit with a side-by-side table. It also discusses missing or added clauses in *Quam oblationem*, the institution narrative and *Unde et memores*. This is direct evidence against treating the entire later Canon as word-for-word unchanged from the fourth century.

Authorship must be labelled precisely: *De sacramentis* is **transmitted under St Ambrose's name**, and older/modern scholars have disputed its authorial relationship to his teaching. The DTC 1929 calls it *Pseudo-Ambroise*, while conceding the case was disputed and the text reflects an Italian liturgical context at the end of the fourth century. Do not attribute all words securely to Ambrose or identify the city, date and exact ordo as established.

## A critical distinction within Genesis 14 — the most important editorial guard

[**Dictionnaire de la Bible, “Melchisédech,” vol. IV pp. 939–942 (1912)**](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_de_la_Bible/Melchis%C3%A9dech) discusses the Hebrew verb rendered *brought forth* bread and wine. It explains that the biblical verb does **not intrinsically mean offering them as sacrifice to God**. It then argues from Melchizedek's status as priest, the surrounding context and patristic interpretation for a sacrificial reading.

This creates three links that **must never be combined into a single falsely literal claim**:

1. **Genesis text** — king-priest Melchizedek brings bread and wine and blesses Abram; a precise geographic pin for the offering is not supplied.
2. **Ancient Christian exegesis** — Cyprian, Ambrose and *De sacramentis* interpret Melchizedek priestly and Eucharistically, invoking Hebrews 7 and the Christian Eucharist.
3. **Received Roman Canon** — *Supra quae* actually speaks of the oblation Melchizedek offered, in the context of recalling three figures.

The Bible dictionary relates Salem to Jerusalem according to substantial commentary, but that **does not certify a precise historical coordinate** for the meeting or an altar. Artwork of Melchizedek must therefore be explicitly an artist's visual representation of biblical and theological tradition.

## Source criticism from French liturgical scholarship

The [DTC Roman liturgy, 1929, pp. 46–55](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_de_th%C3%A9ologie_catholique/MESSE_VIII._La_messe_dans_la_liturgie_10._La_liturgie_romaine) discusses historical change rather than simple continuity. It differentiates early Eucharistic prayer, the later medieval Host and chalice elevations, accompanying bells/candles/incensation, the congregational Amen after the doxology, and variations of Roman liturgical manuscripts. It also describes the prayer *Per quem haec omnia* as possibly retaining traces of former blessings of produce. **None of those timelines, origins or causal chains is regarded as proved simply by reading the 1929 secondary synthesis**. They are recorded as hypotheses or historical claims to check in primary Roman Ordines, sacramentaries and modern textual studies.

The earlier [DTC Canon article, 1909](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_de_th%C3%A9ologie_catholique/CANON_DE_LA_MESSE) gives further discussions of textual variants and origins. Its firm deductions from papal attributions and proposed early epiclesis reconstructions cannot be treated as universally settled. Historical assertions must be checked against newer textual critical work before publication.

Two [original French Le Brun preface pages](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Lebrun_-_Explication_litt%C3%A9rale_historique_et_dogmatique_des_pri%C3%A8res_et_des_c%C3%A9r%C3%A9monies_de_la_messe_-_Tome_1_(1843).djvu/15) support distinct interpretative points:
- At **scanned p. 15**, Le Brun links earthly Eucharistic worship to the Lamb and the heavenly altar and incense of Apocalypse. This is an explicit French author's theological analogy, not proof of a physical geography of heaven.
- At **[scanned p. 30](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Lebrun_-_Explication_litt%C3%A9rale_historique_et_dogmatique_des_pri%C3%A8res_et_des_c%C3%A9r%C3%A9monies_de_la_messe_-_Tome_1_(1843).djvu/30)**, he criticises Dom Claude de Vert's mimetic interpretation of kneeling at the death of Christ in the Passion. Le Brun warns against merely theatrical explanations; compare his own more extensive argument before extrapolating.
