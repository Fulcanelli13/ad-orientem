## EPIP-003 — Epiphany: blessing the home and marking the doorway

*Épiphanie : bénir la maison et marquer la porte*

### English editorial copy

**Title**

Epiphany: blessing the home and marking the doorway

**Description**

At Epiphany some Catholic families mark their doorway with the Cross, the new year and the letters C+M+B. A priest may also be invited to bless the house; that ministerial blessing is distinct from the family's devotional marking.

**Historical evidence**

Francis X. Weiser (1958, p. 149) describes the priestly Epiphany house blessing with the Magnificat, holy water and incense, followed by a chalk inscription on the doorway. Philip Weller's 1952 Roman Ritual translation preserves the priestly formulary. Separately, the Holy See's Directory on Popular Piety (2002, §118) and Irish episcopal household guidance (2022) describe popular or family chalking. The family inscription is not the priest's ritual blessing, and none of these sources makes either practice compulsory.

**Spiritual meaning**

The doorway recalls that Christ is welcomed into the home. The initials can refer to Caspar, Melchior and Balthasar and are also read as Christus mansionem benedicat—may Christ bless this house.

**Household practice**

Where customary, the household may mark the door with a small cross, the year and C+M+B, and pray together at Epiphany. Chalk already blessed by a priest can be used. If a priestly blessing is desired, arrange it with a priest and follow the rite he is authorized to use.

**Cautions and rite boundary**

The family's chalk marking is not the priest's Roman Ritual blessing. Do not present priestly formulas as instructions for lay imitation, assume a parish offers this rite, or claim the inscription automatically protects the house.

**Geographic/ritual scope**

Optional Catholic household custom; regionally variable, with a distinct historical priestly rite

**Calendar association**

Epiphany, 6 January in the 1962 Calendar

## CRIB-001 — Keeping the Christmas crib until Candlemas

*Garder la crèche jusqu'à la Chandeleur*

### English editorial copy

**Title**

Keeping the Christmas crib until Candlemas

**Description**

In some Catholic households, the Nativity crib remains in place until 2 February, the Presentation of the Lord, often called Candlemas. The family then puts it away, marking the close of a cherished cycle of Christmas devotion.

**Historical evidence**

Weiser (1958, p. 300) records Candlemas as the end of the popular Christmas season in many countries and explicitly describes cribs and decorations being stored for the following Christmas. Fr Edward McNamara (2015), discussing Nativity scenes in churches, documents differing local removal dates: after Epiphany, after the Baptism of the Lord, or on 2 February. Historical popular usage and present-day church arrangements are distinct evidence; neither imposes a household rule or changes the 1962 liturgical season.

**Spiritual meaning**

The custom prolongs contemplation of the Incarnation until the feast when Mary and Joseph present Jesus in the Temple, and Simeon welcomes him as light for the nations.

**Household practice**

If the family follows this custom, pray briefly before putting the crib away on Candlemas. Another customary removal date is equally legitimate; no household is required to keep decorations until February.

**Cautions and rite boundary**

Candlemas does not become the formal end of the 1962 Christmas liturgical season because the household crib stays up. This voluntary practice is neither a Mass rubric nor a rule about parish decoration.

**Geographic/ritual scope**

Attested in some European and Catholic household traditions; not universal

**Calendar association**

Candlemas, 2 February; optional household tradition

### Texte français soumis à validation

**Title**

Garder la crèche jusqu'à la Chandeleur

**Description**

Dans certains foyers catholiques, la crèche reste installée jusqu'au 2 février, fête de la Présentation du Seigneur, dite Chandeleur. La famille la range alors, marquant la fin d'une période de dévotion autour de Noël.

**Historical evidence**

Weiser (1958, p. 300) présente la Chandeleur comme la fin de la période populaire de Noël dans de nombreux pays et précise que crèches et décorations sont rangées pour le Noël suivant. À propos des crèches installées dans les églises, le P. Edward McNamara (2015) relève plusieurs dates locales de retrait : après l'Épiphanie, après le Baptême du Seigneur ou le 2 février. L'usage populaire historique et l'organisation actuelle des églises constituent des témoignages distincts : ni l'un ni l'autre n'impose une règle aux foyers ni ne modifie la période liturgique de 1962.

**Spiritual meaning**

Cet usage prolonge la contemplation de l'Incarnation jusqu'à la fête où Marie et Joseph présentent Jésus au Temple, et où Syméon le reconnaît comme lumière des nations.

**Household practice**

Si la famille suit cette coutume, elle peut prier brièvement avant de ranger la crèche à la Chandeleur. Une autre date conforme aux usages locaux est tout aussi légitime ; aucune famille n'est tenue de garder les décorations jusqu'en février.

**Cautions and rite boundary**

Le maintien de la crèche jusqu'à la Chandeleur ne fait pas du 2 février la fin officielle du temps liturgique de Noël dans le calendrier de 1962. Il s'agit d'une coutume libre, non d'une rubrique de la messe ni d'une règle pour les décorations paroissiales.

**Geographic/ritual scope**

Attestée dans certains usages familiaux catholiques, notamment européens ; non universelle

**Calendar association**

Chandeleur, le 2 février ; coutume familiale facultative
