## High-value findings for user-facing Catholic life

1. **Farewell to Alleluia (printed pp. 157–160).** Roman Septuagesima silence is already calendar/liturgy-owned. Weiser reports medieval French popular farewell ceremonies, including the choirboys' burial ritual at Toul and an Alleluia effigy removed in Paris. These are *historical paraliturgical local customs*, **not instructions to stage an authorised 1962 ceremony**. A short background explanation in the Septuagesima guide is useful.
2. **Shrove Tuesday confession (p. 157) and Carnival Forty Hours (p. 167).** Shrovetide confession is historical Catholic preparation; Weiser documents pre-Lent Eucharistic adoration as a response to carnival excesses. The existing Confession and Adoration owners should be linked; no new prayer module, 1958 obligation or universal parish availability.
3. **Laetare and Mothering Sunday (pp. 177–179).** Weiser reports English visits to one's baptismal mother church and one's mother, with simnel cakes. Distinguish this regional older custom from the modern secular holiday and a fixed worldwide Catholic obligation. Laetare's rose vestments already belong to the Calendar.
4. **Historical Lenten hunger cloth (pp. 176–177).** The hungertuch separating congregation from the sanctuary in parts of medieval England, France and Germany could be included as a historical object in a future living-glossary explanation, not silently inserted into a 1962 Mass.
5. **Lenten pretzels and alms (pp. 183–184).** Weiser gives a Christian crossed-arms interpretation and reports their distribution to the poor. Preserve as reported historical interpretation, not an indisputable etymological or Roman-era origin.
6. **Palm Sunday domestic and cemetery customs (pp. 190–192).** France/England cemetery visits at Palm processions, varied local blessed branches (yew, willow, olive), blessed palms shared as reconciliation gestures and sprigs kept near fields or barns. `DOM-013` owns branches kept at home; `DEAD-001` owns cemetery prayers. Regional practice is not universal and blessed branches are not protective talismans.
7. **Holy Thursday seven churches / repositories (pp. 194–195).** Urban visits to decorated altars of repose and Latin `monumento` traditions add genuine context. **The altar of repose is for Eucharistic reservation and prayer, NOT an official depiction of the Holy Sepulchre**. The separate Good Friday Holy Sepulchre shrine described pp. 200–202 is a regional extraliturgical devotional tradition. Do not merge them.
8. **Regional Good Friday processions (pp. 201–203)** in Spain, Malta, Mexico, Latin America and other countries have geographically distinctive scenes, confraternities and statues. They belong with contextual Customs/Explore evidence and direct local attestations, not with instructions inserted into the 1962 Good Friday rite.
9. **Good Friday hot cross buns (p. 206)** are an Anglo-Irish traditional food with a Christian symbol; Weiser also reports beliefs that the buns heal diseases or protect from fire/lightning. Those claims are **superstitions and excluded**.
10. **Easter Vigil fire at home (pp. 216–217).** In parts of Catholic Europe, new domestic light was carried from the blessed church fire by torch, ember or lantern. This is an attractive potential historical story, but the same author reports superstitious lightning-protection claims that must not be adopted. The 1955/1962 Easter Vigil liturgical rite remains Mass/Special Days-owned.
11. **Easter Monday Emmaus walk (p. 223).** Families and friends take an outdoor walk based on Luke 24:13–35; French Canadians historically visited grandparents. This is a strong contextual custom candidate, with local adaptation to climate and cultural practice.
12. **Easter-week pilgrimages (p. 225)**, particularly Friday walks to nearby shrines in Austria/Germany, are existing `DEV-006` pilgrimage material awaiting geographic detail—not a new pilgrimage registry.
13. **Easter blessed-egg family sharing (p. 235–236).** In Poland, Ukraine and Russia, a father divides a blessed egg before Easter breakfast and greets the household. Eggs, paska, lamb and food blessing material must be reconciled with `BLSS-004` in still-open PR #1512, without duplicating that ID.
14. **Ukrainian/Polish pysanky (pp. 234–235)** are complex regional Easter customs and material art; Weiser reports both Christian Easter symbolism and older good-luck motifs. Explain these separately; do not claim automatic blessings from decorative motifs.

## Governing historical distinctions

- **Weiser's Holy Week liturgy reflects the 1955 restoration** introduced by Pius XII. That gives important historical context, but exact 1962 Mass ownership belongs to the canonical app data and separately checked 1962 sources. Medieval rites, obsolete forms and regional para-liturgies are not automatically part of the 1962 Missal.
- **The original 1958 fast, abstinence and indulgence statements are historical snapshots**, not current norms. The catalogue must not create obligations, indulgences, or healing guarantees.
- **The source often compares Latin and Eastern Christian traditions** (Byzantine Holy Shroud, Greek Easter night, Eastern fasting). Preserve them as inter-rite context, do not reassign to the 1962 Roman rite.
- **No superstitions or magical protections:** lucky Easter running water, lightning charms of Easter embers, hot-cross-bun medical claims, fortune-telling and egg symbols promising guaranteed health must remain documentary only or excluded.
- **Do not add Office, hymn or music modules:** the hymnology chapter is valuable historical context but is not product authorisation for new music features.
- **No map pin** from country-level observation or a historical mention. Link verified local places via existing canonical Place IDs only.
